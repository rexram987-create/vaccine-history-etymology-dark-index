Louis Pasteur · 1822–1895

Louis Pasteur

Louis Pasteur did not invent vaccination, but he was one of the most important figures in transforming vaccination into a laboratory-based scientific method grounded in microbiology.

His Place in Vaccine History

Pasteur worked almost a century after Jenner. His contribution was not the invention of vaccination itself, but the creation of a scientific framework for understanding disease agents, weakening them, and developing protective preparations.

He helped move vaccination from clinical observation into the laboratory: identifying pathogens, controlling attenuation, testing preparations systematically, and connecting microorganisms with disease prevention.

Major Vaccine Work

Pasteur and his collaborators worked on experimental vaccines against chicken cholera, anthrax, and rabies. The 1885 rabies work became a landmark because it showed that intervention after exposure could prevent a fatal disease before symptoms appeared.

His work made vaccination not only a medical technique, but a scientific language: a disease agent could be weakened, presented to the immune system, and used to create future protection.

How He Differs from Jenner

Jenner worked from clinical observation and the relationship between cowpox and smallpox. Pasteur worked within a laboratory world of microorganisms, controlled attenuation, and systematic experimentation. Jenner is therefore best described as the father of modern vaccination, while Pasteur is a father of laboratory vaccinology.

Meaning for the Site

Pasteur should be presented as the person who gave vaccination a laboratory and microbiological foundation, but not as the inventor of vaccination itself.

Suggested wording: Pasteur did not invent vaccination; he made it a modern scientific method.
